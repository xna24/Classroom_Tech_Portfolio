The introduction of digital technologies into classrooms has historically opened up new possibilities for both teacher-centred (presentation, animation, educational videos etc.) and student-centred activities (e.g., interactive features in applets such as GeoGebra in mathematics). Technologies which had become widely available often spark debates and changes in curricula and assessment, about what knowledge and skill should remain a core part of what we want students to learn and what is becoming obsolete.

As a pre-service math teacher and a former math student, I experienced the cultural shock of interacting with both calculator-educated students and those like myself who were brought up doing arithmetic purely manually. To me, the use of calculators in a math classroom can either be an outsourcing of cognitive capacity or an opportunity to steer students’ finite energy away from tedious calculation and toward a deeper conceptual understanding. I believe that the case of digital technologies along with its newest variant, the AI “revolution”, should be approached in a similar fashion. Technologies themselves are not pure good or pure evil. A good educator should strive to take full advantage of the new possibilities afforded by technologies, but at the same time be very cautious of its potential harm, its influence on young people as well as the wider societal impact. This attitude toward technology should also be modelled for our students as they grow up to be future citizens of our democracy.

As classroom teachers, our influence on shaping how technologies are incorporated in teaching is somewhat limited. We are often under the pressure from policy-makers and school authorities, and sometimes under peer-pressure to adopt the newest technology and I’m pretty sure the push for AI in classroom is waiting right around the corner. This is why I prefer a school-based/community-based approach in regards to education technology, in evaluating and standardising guidelines for adaptation and collaborating in developing teaching resources and providing opportunities for teacher professional development.
Another alarming trend of education technology is that end-users (teachers and students) tend to own less and less of the means of producing, controlling or customising the tools/resources (quite often overpriced, subscription-based, produced and pushed by Big Tech). I hope to see more open-source, open-access digital platforms and resources in education in the future.
