### GenAI Response 1

<div class="genai-portal" markdown="1">

A substantially different redesign would shift the lesson from **teacher explanation + individual textbook practice + optional digital visualisation** toward a **collaborative, language-supported, physically manipulable investigation**. The key is to preserve the analytical demand while reducing unnecessary barriers caused by language, attention, working memory, and technology access.

## Year 9 Mathematics — Surface Area of Right Prisms and Cylinders

**Australian Curriculum v9:** AC9M9M01
**Duration:** 55 minutes
**Topic:** Surface area of right prisms and cylinders
**Cognitive demand:** Analyse → justify → apply
**Technology:** Not required; one teacher device/projector can be used if available.

### Learning intention

> We are learning to analyse how the faces of a 3D object contribute to its surface area and use this relationship to solve problems involving right prisms and cylinders.

### Success criteria

By the end of the lesson, students can:

1. **Identify and classify** the 2D faces that make up the surface of a right prism or cylinder.
2. **Analyse a net** to determine which areas contribute to total surface area.
3. **Explain the relationship** between the dimensions of a solid and its surface area.
4. **Develop/use a formula** for surface area and justify why it works.
5. Solve a surface-area problem using appropriate units and communicate their reasoning.

The success criteria deliberately go beyond "substitute values into a formula": students need to **analyse the structure of the solid and explain relationships**.

---

# Lesson sequence — 55 minutes

| Time      | Phase                                  | Student activity                                  | Teacher role                                  | Inclusion focus                                     |
| --------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| 0–5 min   | **Engage / retrieve**                  | Predict and discuss the faces of familiar objects | Model vocabulary and activate prior knowledge | Visuals, oral rehearsal, movement                   |
| 5–13 min  | **I do: build the idea**               | Observe a physical object being "unwrapped"       | Model net → faces → area                      | Explicit language + concrete representation         |
| 13–25 min | **We do: investigate**                 | Groups construct/analyse prism and cylinder nets  | Question, scaffold, check understanding       | Collaborative roles, manipulatives, structured talk |
| 25–37 min | **We do: analyse and derive**          | Compare changing dimensions and infer formulas    | Facilitate reasoning rather than give formula | EAL/D sentence frames, ADHD chunking                |
| 37–49 min | **You do: differentiated application** | Solve and explain an unfamiliar problem           | Confer and provide targeted scaffolds         | Choice of representation and scaffold level         |
| 49–55 min | **Exit ticket / synthesis**            | Individually explain one relationship             | Assess conceptual understanding               | Short, predictable, low-writing assessment          |

---

# 1. Engage and retrieve — 0–5 minutes

Place three objects where students can see them:

* a rectangular cereal/milk carton
* a cylindrical can
* a triangular-prism object or folded triangular-prism net

The objects do not need to be commercially packaged specifically for the lesson; cardboard constructions are sufficient.

Ask:

> **"If I wanted to cover the entire outside of this object with paper, what would I need to know?"**

Students first **think silently**, then discuss with a partner.

Rather than immediately introducing *surface area*, display:

**cover → outside → faces → area → total**

Ask students to connect the words.

### EAL/D support

Keep a small visual vocabulary strip on each table:

| Word         | Visual/meaning                  |
| ------------ | ------------------------------- |
| face         | flat surface                    |
| edge         | where two faces meet            |
| net          | 3D object opened out flat       |
| radius       | centre to edge of circle        |
| height       | perpendicular distance          |
| surface area | total area of all outside faces |

Students are not expected to learn the terminology through verbal explanation alone. Each word is paired with a **diagram, gesture or physical example**.

### ADHD support

The opening has a very short cycle:

**look → think → talk → share**

rather than a prolonged teacher introduction.

---

# 2. I do: physically unwrap the object — 5–13 minutes

Instead of beginning with a diagram of a net, physically demonstrate one.

For example, take a small cardboard cuboid and carefully open it out.

Ask:

> "What happened to the 3D object?"

Students identify that:

* the object stayed made from the same surfaces;
* the surfaces became 2D shapes;
* the total area of those shapes represents the area of the outside of the original object.

Draw the corresponding net on the board.

Then deliberately **colour-code the reasoning**:

**Cuboid**

* top → rectangle
* bottom → rectangle
* front → rectangle
* back → rectangle
* left → rectangle
* right → rectangle

Then ask:

> "Do I really need to calculate six completely different areas?"

Students notice that opposite faces are congruent.

This provides the bridge from their existing knowledge of **area of 2D shapes** to surface area.

### Important change from the original lesson

The teacher does **not immediately give the formula**.

Instead:

> **3D object → net → component faces → areas → total**

becomes the conceptual structure that students will later use to reconstruct the formula.

---

# 3. Collaborative investigation — 13–25 minutes

This is the central redesign.

Give each group a small **"surface area investigation kit"**:

* one cardboard cuboid
* one cardboard cylinder
* pre-cut nets
* scissors/tape if appropriate
* ruler
* string
* measuring tape
* calculator
* investigation card

You could make the solids very cheaply from cereal boxes, paper cups, cardboard tubes and scrap cardboard.

### Group roles

For ADHD and equitable participation, assign four short roles:

**Builder** — manipulates the model
**Measurer** — measures dimensions
**Recorder** — records the group's reasoning
**Reporter** — prepares the explanation

Roles rotate after the prism investigation.

This is preferable to simply saying "work in groups", because students have a clearly defined action and responsibility.

---

## Investigation A — Cuboid

Students open the cuboid net and complete:

> **1. How many faces are there?**
> **2. What shape is each face?**
> **3. Which faces have equal areas?**
> **4. Calculate the area of each different type of face.**
> **5. How can you combine these areas efficiently?**

Then:

> **Explain why your method gives the total surface area.**

The final question is important: it requires **analysis and justification**, rather than procedural substitution.

Students should be led toward:

$$
SA=2lw+2lh+2wh
$$

but the formula is presented as a **summary of their reasoning**, not as the starting point.

---

# 4. Cylinder investigation — 25–37 minutes

Now give students a cylinder net.

Rather than simply telling them that the rectangle's dimensions are \(h\) and \(2\pi r\), make this the central analytical question:

> **"The curved part of the cylinder becomes a rectangle. What must the width of this rectangle be?"**

Students use string to wrap around the circular base.

They discover:

$$
\text{circumference}=2\pi r
$$

Therefore the rectangular part of the net has dimensions:

$$
h \times 2\pi r
$$

and its area is:

$$
2\pi rh
$$

The two circular ends have total area:

$$
2\pi r^2
$$

leading students to reconstruct:

$$
\boxed{SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh}
$$

### Key analytical discussion

Put these two representations beside each other:

**Cylinder**

$$
\underbrace{\pi r^2+\pi r^2}_{\text{two circular ends}}
+
\underbrace{(2\pi r)h}_{\text{curved surface}}
$$

Ask:

> "Which part of the formula belongs to which part of the net?"

Then deliberately change one dimension:

**Cylinder A:** same \(r\), greater \(h\)
**Cylinder B:** same \(h\), greater \(r\)

Ask:

> "Which part of the surface area changes, and why?"

This is an especially useful **Analyse-level task**, because students must reason about the structure of the formula rather than merely calculate it.

---

# 5. "Which cylinder has more surface area?" — 37–49 minutes

Rather than a conventional set of ten textbook exercises, use **three problems with increasing analytical complexity**.

Students choose or are assigned an appropriate starting point.

### Problem 1 — Analyse a net

A cylinder has radius \(4\) cm and height \(10\) cm.

Students:

1. sketch or label its net;
2. identify the dimensions of every component;
3. calculate its surface area;
4. annotate their working to show where each term in the formula comes from.

This establishes the basic conceptual structure.

---

### Problem 2 — Compare

Two cylinders have:

**Cylinder A:** \(r=3\) cm, \(h=12\) cm
**Cylinder B:** \(r=6\) cm, \(h=6\) cm

Ask:

> **Without calculating the complete surface areas immediately, predict which parts of the surface area will differ. Explain your prediction.**

Then calculate and check.

This is much more cognitively demanding than routine substitution because students must analyse the formula before calculating.

---

### Problem 3 — Error analysis

Give students this student's solution:

> "The surface area of a cylinder with radius 5 cm and height 8 cm is
>
> $$
> 2\pi(5)(8)+\pi(5)^2
> $$
>
> because the cylinder has one circular end and one curved surface."

Ask:

> **"What is wrong with this reasoning? Correct it and explain how you know."**

This is particularly valuable for the new cohort because it allows students to demonstrate mathematical reasoning without requiring a long written response.

Expected insight:

A cylinder has **two** circular faces, so the circular contribution is \(2\pi r^2\).

---

# 6. Exit ticket — 49–55 minutes

Students independently complete a very short exit ticket.

### Exit ticket

**A cylinder has radius \(r\) and height \(h\).**

**A.** What are the three parts of its surface area?

**B.** Explain why the curved part has area \(2\pi rh\).

**C.** A student says:

> "If I double the height but keep the radius the same, the whole surface area doubles."

Do you agree? **Explain your reasoning.**

Part C is deliberately analytical. Students need to recognise that only the **curved surface component** doubles; the two circular ends remain unchanged.

For students who have difficulty writing extended English, allow:

* labelled diagrams;
* mathematical notation;
* a short oral explanation to the teacher;
* sentence completion.

The mathematical expectation remains high; the **language demand is scaffolded rather than the mathematical demand lowered**.

---

# Supporting EAL/D learners without reducing the mathematics

A particularly important principle here is to distinguish **mathematical complexity** from **English-language complexity**.

For example, avoid asking:

> "Describe comprehensively the relationship between the dimensions of the cylinder and its surface area."

when the actual mathematical task is simply to analyse how changing \(h\) affects the formula.

Instead provide a sentence frame:

> "When the ______ increases, the ______ part of the surface area changes because ______."

Or:

> "The rectangle in the cylinder's net has dimensions ______ and ______ because ______."

### Three levels of language support

**Level 1 — visual**

Diagram + labelled vocabulary.

**Level 2 — sentence frame**

> "The ______ face has area ______ because ______."

**Level 3 — independent explanation**

> "Explain how the net can be used to derive the surface-area formula."

Students can move between levels without being permanently placed into a "low" group.

### Allow multiple modes of mathematical communication

Students can demonstrate understanding through:

* drawing;
* pointing and manipulating a net;
* mathematical notation;
* oral explanation;
* labelled diagrams;
* short written explanations.

This is especially useful because an EAL/D student's difficulty expressing a sophisticated mathematical idea in English should not automatically be interpreted as difficulty with the mathematics itself.

---

# ADHD-responsive design

The lesson deliberately avoids requiring students to sustain attention through a long explanation.

Instead, the rhythm is approximately:

**5 min — engage**
↓
**8 min — teacher modelling**
↓
**12 min — hands-on investigation**
↓
**12 min — collaborative reasoning**
↓
**12 min — individual application**
↓
**6 min — exit ticket**

### Additional adjustments

**1. Visible agenda**

Keep a simple board display:

> **1. Look**
> **2. Unwrap**
> **3. Find the areas**
> **4. Explain the formula**
> **5. Solve**

Cross off each stage as the lesson progresses.

**2. Chunk instructions**

Give no more than 2–3 instructions at once.

Instead of:

> "Take the cylinder, measure the radius, calculate the circumference, identify the rectangle's dimensions and then calculate the total area..."

use:

> **Step 1:** Find the radius.
> **Step 2:** Wrap the string around the circle.
> **Step 3:** Compare the string with the rectangle.

**3. Build in purposeful movement**

Students physically:

* unwrap nets;
* measure;
* rotate solids;
* use string;
* move between a model and its net.

Movement is therefore part of the mathematical task rather than a distraction from it.

**4. Reduce copying**

Provide the investigation sheet with diagrams and partially completed tables. Students spend their attention on **reasoning**, not copying questions from the board.

**5. Short feedback cycles**

Rather than waiting until the end of the worksheet, the teacher checks understanding after each conceptual step.

---

# Low-technology replacement for the GeoGebra activity

I would **not try to replicate GeoGebra digitally** given the technology constraint.

Instead, retain its strongest pedagogical feature—**manipulating the relationship between a 3D solid and its net**—using physical models.

### "Cylinder transformation" cards

Prepare several cylinder cards:

| Cylinder | Radius | Height |
| -------- | -----: | -----: |
| A        |   3 cm |   8 cm |
| B        |   3 cm |  12 cm |
| C        |   5 cm |   8 cm |
| D        |   5 cm |  12 cm |

Groups receive the corresponding nets or diagrams.

Ask them to physically compare:

> **What changed?**
> **What stayed the same?**
> **Which part of the net changed?**
> **Which term in the formula changed?**

This actually makes an important mathematical relationship more explicit than simply manipulating a digital slider.

If the classroom has a projector, the teacher can optionally demonstrate the existing GeoGebra model for **2–3 minutes**, but it is an enhancement rather than a prerequisite.

---

# Differentiation without lowering cognitive demand

A useful structure is to vary the **scaffolding**, not the intellectual goal.

| Student need                          | Scaffold                                          | Mathematical demand |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Significant EAL/D                     | labelled diagrams, vocabulary, sentence frames    | Analyse             |
| EAL/D with stronger English           | fewer sentence frames                             | Analyse/justify     |
| ADHD                                  | short steps, physical models, assigned roles      | Analyse             |
| Needs additional mathematical support | partially completed net/formula                   | Analyse             |
| Ready for extension                   | compare changing \(r\) and \(h\), critique claims | Analyse/evaluate    |

### Extension

Ask:

> "A student claims that doubling the radius always doubles the surface area of a cylinder. Is the claim true? Use the formula to investigate."

Students should recognise that:

$$
SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh
$$

contains both \(r^2\) and \(r\), so changing radius has a different effect from changing height.

That provides genuine extension without simply giving more difficult arithmetic.

---

# Assessment strategy

Rather than relying primarily on textbook completion, collect evidence at **three points**.

### Formative assessment 1 — questioning

During the net investigation:

> "How do you know this rectangle represents the curved surface?"

Look for conceptual reasoning, not just the answer.

### Formative assessment 2 — group explanation

Listen for whether students can connect:

**3D face → 2D shape → dimensions → area → total surface area**

### Formative assessment 3 — exit ticket

The final question distinguishes:

* students who can calculate;
* students who understand the formula;
* students who can **analyse how changing dimensions affects surface area**.

That distinction is particularly important given the requirement to maintain at least **Bloom's Analyse level**.

---

## What is substantially different about this redesign?

The original sequence can be characterised as:

> **Familiar objects → nets → teacher derivation → textbook practice**

The redesigned lesson becomes:

> **Physical experience → collaborative investigation → analyse the structure of nets → derive formulas → compare changing dimensions → critique reasoning**

The major change is therefore not simply "add EAL/D scaffolds" or "make it more ADHD-friendly". **The pedagogy itself changes.**

The **net becomes the central mathematical object**, while the formula is the *result of analysing the net*. Physical manipulation replaces the assumption that every student can construct the same mental 3D image from a static diagram or individual digital simulation.

Most importantly, the lesson maintains the same curriculum outcome and mathematical sophistication: students are still expected to **analyse, derive, justify and apply**, while barriers related to English proficiency, sustained attention and unequal access to technology are reduced.

### GenAI Response 2

<div class="genai-portal" markdown="1">
Yes. I think your concern is well founded: **sentence frames can easily shift from reducing linguistic barriers to reducing the mathematical reasoning required**. For this particular comparison problem, I would make a sharper distinction between **scaffolding the mode of expression** and **scaffolding the mathematical thinking itself**.

The UDL-aligned adjustment I would recommend is therefore:

> **Keep the reasoning task completely open, but broaden the ways students can construct and communicate that reasoning.**

The student should still have to decide *what changes, what stays constant, which components matter, and how that supports the comparison*. What changes is the representational pathway through which they demonstrate that reasoning.

## 1. Reframe the task

Instead of:

> **Cylinder A:** \(r=3\) cm, \(h=12\) cm
> **Cylinder B:** \(r=6\) cm, \(h=6\) cm
>
> Without calculating the complete surface areas immediately, predict which cylinder has more surface area. Explain your prediction.

I would retain the mathematical task but change the instruction to:

> **Compare the two cylinders. Use one or more representations to show your reasoning.**
>
> You may use:
>
> * a labelled sketch or net
> * annotations/arrows
> * mathematical expressions
> * calculations
> * physical manipulation of the nets
> * words in English or another familiar language
> * oral explanation
> * a combination of these.
>
> **Your reasoning must make clear why your evidence supports your prediction.**

That final sentence is important. It keeps the **epistemic requirement** intact: students must establish a relationship between evidence and conclusion.

---

# 2. What should *not* be scaffolded away?

For this problem, I would identify four mathematical ideas that students need to coordinate:

### A. Decompose the surface area

Students need to recognise:

$$
SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh
$$

or its equivalent net representation.

### B. Notice that both variables change

Here:

$$
r:3\rightarrow6
$$

but

$$
h:12\rightarrow6
$$

So this isn't simply a "bigger dimensions = bigger surface area" question.

### C. Analyse the different effects

Doubling \(r\) affects:

$$
2\pi r^2
$$

and also:

$$
2\pi rh
$$

while halving \(h\) affects only the curved-surface component.

### D. Connect evidence to the conclusion

Students need to establish *why* their analysis supports "A" or "B".

Those four things should **not** be supplied in a sentence frame.

For example, I would avoid:

> "Cylinder ___ has a larger surface area because its ___ is larger, while its ___ is smaller."

That is linguistically supportive, but it has already told the student much of the mathematical structure they are supposed to discover.

---

# 3. Instead, separate "what you think" from "how you show it"

This is where I think UDL becomes particularly powerful.

Give students an **open reasoning canvas** rather than a sentence-completion worksheet.

For example:

### Compare the cylinders

**Cylinder A**

\(r=3\) cm
\(h=12\) cm

**Cylinder B**

\(r=6\) cm
\(h=6\) cm

### Your prediction

**A / B / I need more information**

### Show your reasoning

There is a large blank space containing small icons:

**[net] [draw] [calculate] [annotate] [formula] [explain]**

Students can choose which representations they need.

The teacher's instruction is simply:

> **"Convince someone that your conclusion makes sense."**

That is much less linguistically restrictive than a sentence frame, but arguably **more cognitively demanding**.

---

# 4. An especially useful option: "mathematical annotation"

For EAL/D learners, I would explicitly teach that **mathematical reasoning does not have to be a paragraph**.

For example, a student might produce something like:

$$
SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh
$$

then annotate:

$$
r:3\rightarrow6
$$

$$
h:12\rightarrow6
$$

and draw arrows to the two terms:

$$
2\pi r^2 \quad \uparrow
$$

$$
2\pi rh \quad ?
$$

They might then calculate:

$$
A:\quad 18\pi+72\pi=90\pi
$$

$$
B:\quad72\pi+72\pi=144\pi
$$

and write:

> **B > A**

That is a legitimate mathematical argument.

The student has analysed the structure, tracked the changing quantities, calculated the relevant components and established a conclusion—even if their English proficiency does not yet allow them to produce an elegant explanatory paragraph.

**The mathematical reasoning has not been simplified. Only the linguistic bottleneck has been reduced.**

---

# 5. Use "representation choice", not "easier version"

I would make this explicit to students.

For example:

> **Everyone must answer the same mathematical question. You choose how to convince us.**

Students could choose:

### Option A — Diagrammatic

Draw both nets and annotate what changes.

### Option B — Algebraic

Use the surface-area expression to compare the two cylinders.

### Option C — Concrete

Manipulate the physical nets and use annotations/photos/sketches to show which components change.

### Option D — Numerical

Calculate the component areas and compare them.

### Option E — Multimodal

Combine any of the above with a short oral or written explanation.

These aren't five levels of difficulty. They are **five routes to demonstrating the same underlying reasoning**.

That distinction is important for avoiding inadvertent ability grouping.

---

# 6. A particularly strong EAL/D strategy: allow multilingual reasoning

I would go one step further than simply allowing "another familiar language".

For students who are still developing academic English, allow the **initial reasoning to occur in the language in which they can think mathematically most fluently**.

For example, a student might:

1. discuss the comparison with a peer in their shared language;
2. annotate a diagram using mathematical notation;
3. use English mathematical vocabulary where they know it;
4. give a brief English conclusion if appropriate.

The teacher can still identify whether the student understands:

> radius → circular areas → curved surface → total surface area

without confusing **English proficiency with mathematical proficiency**.

This is especially relevant to assessment. If the lesson's learning intention is surface area, we don't want an English-language production task to become an unintended second assessment criterion.

---

# 7. But don't remove the opportunity to develop mathematical English

There is an important nuance here.

I wouldn't interpret UDL as:

> "EAL/D students shouldn't have to use English."

Rather:

> **Students should have access to the mathematical idea before being required to express that idea in increasingly sophisticated English.**

So I would use a **two-stage response**.

### Stage 1 — demonstrate the reasoning

Any combination of:

* diagram
* net
* notation
* calculation
* home language
* gesture/manipulation
* English

### Stage 2 — optional/targeted language development

Once the reasoning is established, the teacher can ask:

> "Can you give me one mathematical sentence that describes your reasoning?"

Now the language task is **layered onto an established mathematical idea**, rather than becoming a prerequisite for demonstrating that idea.

For example:

> "Cylinder B has greater surface area because ______."

That's appropriate as a *language-development opportunity*, but I wouldn't make the sentence frame the primary mechanism through which students are expected to discover the mathematics.

---

# 8. Use a "reasoning checklist" rather than a sentence frame

This may be the most useful practical replacement for your original sentence-completion idea.

Instead of:

> "Cylinder ___ has greater surface area because..."

give students:

### Have I shown...

☐ what happens to \(r\)?
☐ what happens to \(h\)?
☐ which parts of the surface area are affected?
☐ evidence for my comparison?
☐ why my evidence supports my conclusion?

This is a **metacognitive scaffold**, rather than a mathematical scaffold.

It tells students *what a convincing argument needs to contain* without telling them **how to reason their way to the answer**.

That's a much better fit for maintaining Analyse-level demand.

---

# 9. Physical manipulation can actually support deeper analysis

There's also an interesting connection to your original GeoGebra idea.

Suppose each group has the two cylinder nets.

Students can physically overlay or compare:

**A**

* two smaller circles
* long rectangle

**B**

* two larger circles
* shorter rectangle

Then ask:

> **"What changed in the net?"**

Students can mark changes with arrows.

For example:

**circles:** larger ↑
**rectangle:** wider ↑ but shorter ↓

That creates a visual representation of the competing effects.

The crucial next question is:

> **"Does one change automatically mean the whole surface area is larger?"**

Now students are being forced to reason about the **interaction between components**, rather than relying on the superficial heuristic that "bigger radius means bigger surface area".

So the physical representation isn't a lower-level alternative to the algebra. It can actually be the **representation through which sophisticated analysis becomes accessible**.

---

# 10. You can deliberately triangulate representations

For this particular activity, I would use a progression like:

**physical net**

↓

**visual/diagrammatic representation**

↓

**mathematical representation**

↓

**verbal explanation**

But I would **not require every student to produce all four independently**.

Instead, the teacher can deliberately connect them:

> "Show me on your net which part this \(2\pi r^2\) represents."

> "Now show me on the formula what changed when the radius doubled."

> "Can you point to the part of your diagram that supports that?"

> "Can you give me the mathematical word for that?"

This lets students move between representations while the teacher assesses whether they understand the underlying relationship.

---

# 11. What this might look like in the actual lesson

I would revise the original **37–49 minute comparison activity** to something like this:

### 37–40 min — Present the problem

Display:

> **Cylinder A:** \(r=3,\ h=12\)
> **Cylinder B:** \(r=6,\ h=6\)
>
> **Which has the greater surface area?**
>
> **Do not calculate the complete surface area yet. First investigate.**

Give each group the two nets.

---

### 40–44 min — Explore

Students manipulate, sketch, annotate, discuss, calculate partial quantities, or use the formula.

Teacher circulates and asks **non-leading questions**:

> "What changed?"

> "What stayed the same?"

> "Which parts of the net are affected?"

> "How do you know?"

> "Where can we see that in the formula?"

Notice that none of these questions supplies the reasoning.

---

### 44–47 min — Construct an argument

Students create a response under:

> **"Convince another mathematician."**

They choose their representation.

A response could be a diagram, mathematical working, annotations, oral explanation, or combination.

---

### 47–49 min — Compare reasoning

Select two genuinely different representations.

For example:

**Student 1:** algebraic comparison
**Student 2:** net-based comparison

Ask the class:

> **"These look different. Are they actually showing the same mathematical idea?"**

This is itself an excellent Analyse-level discussion because students must identify the correspondence between representations.

---

# 12. One further adjustment I would make to the assessment

I'd avoid marking the student's **English quality** when the stated learning outcome is mathematical.

A simple assessment rubric could separate the dimensions:

| Criterion                   | Evidence                                                         |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mathematical identification | Identifies relevant components of cylinder surface               |
| Analysis                    | Identifies how changing \(r\) and \(h\) affects those components |
| Justification               | Uses evidence to support comparison                              |
| Representation              | Makes reasoning mathematically interpretable                     |
| Mathematical language       | Uses relevant terminology where appropriate                      |

The first three are central to the mathematical learning outcome.

The final criterion can be used **formatively for EAL/D language development**, rather than allowing limited English proficiency to obscure evidence of mathematical understanding.

---

## The key UDL principle here

I would summarise the adjustment as:

> **Don't make the mathematics easier; make the route to demonstrating the mathematics more flexible.**

For this activity, that means **removing English as an unnecessary gatekeeper to Analyse-level reasoning**, while still giving students opportunities to develop the English needed to communicate mathematics.

So I would replace **sentence completion** with **open-ended multimodal mathematical argument + a reasoning checklist**.

That combination gives students substantial freedom over *how* they communicate while keeping the intellectual burden firmly on *what they need to figure out*.

### GenAI Response 3

<div class="genai-portal" markdown="1">
I would revise my earlier recommendation. The absence of 1:1 devices does **not** imply that digital technology has little pedagogical value. In this lesson, GeoGebra has affordances that cardboard models cannot fully reproduce—particularly **continuous variation, rapid reversibility, simultaneous linking of the 3D solid and its net, and making relationships between variables visible**.

The important question is therefore not:

> "Digital or physical?"

but:

> **"How can one shared digital resource become an interactive learning environment rather than a teacher demonstration?"**

For this cohort, I would use a **hybrid model**: physical manipulatives for distributed hands-on access, plus a shared GeoGebra model for dynamic investigation.

---

# 1. What GeoGebra adds that the physical model does not

The physical cylinder remains valuable, particularly for EAL/D learners because students can literally see and touch the correspondence between the solid and its net.

But the digital model can do several things much more efficiently.

### Physical model

Students can investigate:

* What does a cylinder's net look like?
* How does the curved surface become a rectangle?
* Which dimensions correspond?
* What are the two circular faces?

### Dynamic digital model

Students can investigate:

* What happens **continuously** as \(r\) changes?
* What happens **continuously** as \(h\) changes?
* Which parts of the net change?
* Which parts stay fixed?
* Does surface area always increase in the same way?
* How do changes in \(r\) and \(h\) interact?
* Can we predict what will happen before moving the slider?

That last category is where I think the technology is particularly valuable.

A physical model is excellent for **seeing the structure**; a dynamic model is excellent for **analysing variation**.

---

# 2. I would therefore retain GeoGebra—but change its role

I would not use it as:

> Teacher explains cylinder → teacher opens GeoGebra → teacher manipulates sliders → students watch.

That is essentially a digital version of "I do".

Instead, make GeoGebra a **shared investigation tool**.

The teacher should deliberately *not* control the mathematical decisions.

Students do.

For example:

> **"We have one minute. Decide what you want me to change in the model and predict what will happen before I touch the slider."**

The teacher becomes the **operator**, while students become the **mathematical decision-makers**.

This distinction is quite important.

---

# 3. One-device interaction can still be genuinely collaborative

Suppose the projector displays a GeoGebra cylinder with:

* a 3D cylinder;
* its unfolded net;
* a slider for \(r\);
* a slider for \(h\);
* numerical surface-area output.

Give each group two small cards:

**PREDICT**

**TEST**

The teacher presents:

> \(r=3,\ h=8\)

and asks:

> "If I increase \(r\), what do you predict will happen to the net?"

Groups discuss and hold up their prediction.

Then one nominated student tells the operator:

> "Increase the radius to 5."

The operator does so.

Now the class compares the prediction with the result.

The crucial instructional cycle is:

**Predict → manipulate → observe → explain → revise**

rather than:

**Watch → copy → calculate**

That makes the technology interactive even though only one person physically controls the device.

---

# 4. Even better: make the students issue the commands

This is a subtle but useful redesign.

Instead of the teacher saying:

> "Now let's increase the radius."

ask:

> **"What should we change if we want to investigate whether radius or height has the greater effect?"**

A student might say:

> "Keep the height at 10 and increase the radius."

The teacher then operates the slider.

The mathematical decision has therefore been made by students.

You can go further:

> "Tell me exactly what values I should test."

For example, students may decide:

$$
r=2,\quad4,\quad6
$$

while keeping:

$$
h=10
$$

Now the class has effectively designed its own experiment.

That is considerably more powerful than a teacher demonstration.

---

# 5. Combine this with the EAL/D multimodal approach

This is where the digital and UDL redesigns can reinforce each other.

Students don't have to explain their prediction in an extended English sentence.

Give each group a small **prediction board**:

### We predict...

**Radius:** ↑ / ↓ / same

**Height:** ↑ / ↓ / same

**Circular part:** ↑ / ↓ / same

**Curved part:** ↑ / ↓ / same

**Total surface area:** ↑ / ↓ / same

**Our evidence:**
[diagram / notation / words / gesture / calculation]

Students can communicate their mathematical prediction through combinations of symbols, arrows, diagrams, mathematical notation and language.

Then the class uses GeoGebra to test the prediction.

This is particularly nice because the **dynamic visualisation supplies shared referents**. An EAL/D learner does not have to construct an English description of an abstract change that the class cannot see. Everyone can see the cylinder and net changing simultaneously.

---

# 6. Revised 55-minute lesson

I would now structure the lesson like this.

## 0–5 min — Activate prior knowledge

Use the physical cuboid and cylinder.

Ask:

> "If we covered the outside completely, what would we need to calculate?"

Briefly retrieve:

* face
* area
* net
* radius
* height

No technology yet.

The physical objects establish the conceptual foundation.

---

## 5–12 min — Physical "unwrapping"

Physically open the cuboid and cylinder nets.

Students identify:

$$
\text{3D solid}\rightarrow\text{2D faces}\rightarrow\text{areas}
$$

For the cylinder, students physically use string to connect the circumference of the circle with the width of the rectangular part of the net.

Establish:

$$
C=2\pi r
$$

and therefore:

$$
A_{\text{curved}}=(2\pi r)h
$$

The physical model handles the **static structural relationship**.

---

# 7. 12–22 min — Shared GeoGebra investigation

Now bring up the interactive model.

The first objective is not calculating surface area.

It is:

> **"What changes when the dimensions change?"**

### Round 1: radius

Start with:

$$
r=3,\quad h=8
$$

Ask:

> "What do you predict will happen if I increase \(r\) while keeping \(h\) constant?"

Groups record predictions using arrows/diagrams/notation.

Then a student directs the operation.

The class observes:

* the circular faces enlarge;
* the rectangle changes in width;
* the rectangle's height stays constant;
* the total surface area changes.

### Round 2: height

Reset.

Ask:

> "Now what happens if \(h\) changes but \(r\) stays constant?"

Again:

**predict → student instruction → manipulate → observe**

Students should notice that the rectangle changes in its vertical dimension, while the circular faces remain unchanged.

---

# 8. 22–30 min — From dynamic model to formula

Now freeze the model.

Ask:

> "Which parts of the formula correspond to which parts of the net?"

Display:

$$
SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh
$$

Students come to the board or point to the projected model:

$$
\boxed{2\pi r^2}
$$

→ two circular faces

and

$$
\boxed{2\pi rh}
$$

→ rectangular curved surface.

This creates a powerful **multiple-representation connection**:

**3D model ↔ net ↔ algebraic expression**

The teacher is now explicitly helping students coordinate representations, rather than merely showing them one after another.

---

# 9. 30–41 min — Student-designed digital investigation

This is where I would make the biggest change from my first redesign.

Give groups a challenge:

> **"Design a test that helps us answer this question: Does changing the radius have the same effect on surface area as changing the height?"**

They must decide:

* what to hold constant;
* what to change;
* which values to test;
* what they predict;
* what evidence they need.

For example, a group might propose:

$$
r=3,\quad h=10
$$

then:

$$
r=6,\quad h=10
$$

and compare that with:

$$
r=3,\quad h=20.
$$

The teacher enters the values into GeoGebra as directed by the class.

### Why this is better than individual-device GeoGebra

The scarce device becomes a **shared scientific/mathematical instrument**.

Students aren't waiting for a turn to click randomly. They are collectively deciding:

> **What should we manipulate, and why?**

This gives the single device a legitimate pedagogical role.

---

# 10. 41–49 min — Return to the comparison problem

Now bring back the earlier problem:

$$
A:\quad r=3,\ h=12
$$

$$
B:\quad r=6,\ h=6
$$

Ask:

> **"Which cylinder has the greater surface area? Convince another mathematician."**

But now students have three available resources:

### Physical

Their nets/models.

### Digital

The dynamic GeoGebra representation that the class has just investigated.

### Symbolic

$$
SA=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh
$$

Students choose how to construct their argument.

This is where the earlier UDL adjustment fits particularly well.

A student might produce:

$$
SA_A=18\pi+72\pi=90\pi
$$

$$
SA_B=72\pi+72\pi=144\pi
$$

Another might draw the two nets and annotate which components become larger/smaller.

Another might use the formula and arrows.

Another might orally explain while pointing to the relevant components of the projected net.

**Same mathematical task; multiple means of representation and expression.**

---

# 11. 49–55 min — Individual exit ticket

I would now make the exit ticket explicitly exploit the dynamic investigation.

### Part A

> When the radius increases while height stays constant, identify **two parts of the surface area representation that change**.

Students may answer using a diagram, notation or words.

### Part B

> When the height increases while radius stays constant, which part of the surface area changes? Show how you know.

### Part C

> A student says:
>
> **"Doubling the height doubles the surface area."**
>
> Use a representation of your choice to investigate the claim.

This is stronger than simply asking students to calculate a surface area.

They need to analyse the claim against the structure of the formula/net.

---

# 12. How the one-device setup could work operationally

You don't actually need every student to have direct access to GeoGebra.

I'd establish a few predictable roles:

### Whole-class "control room"

At the front:

**Operator**
One student physically controls the laptop.

**Mathematical director**
Another student/group tells the operator what to change.

**Prediction reporters**
Groups hold up or display their predicted result.

**Evidence checker**
A student identifies what changed in the model.

Rotate these roles every few minutes.

This creates **distributed participation around the single device**.

The important point is that operating the mouse is not the learning objective. If 25 students are watching one student drag a slider, that's still a demonstration.

If 25 students are **predicting, deciding what to manipulate, interpreting the result and revising their models**, the single device becomes a shared interactive environment.

---

# 13. There is also a useful "paper interface" to the digital model

Given the limited technology budget, I'd prepare a set of laminated cards:

**CHANGE \(r\)**
**CHANGE \(h\)**
**KEEP \(r\) CONSTANT**
**KEEP \(h\) CONSTANT**
**PREDICT**
**TEST**
**EXPLAIN**

Groups can physically arrange the cards to design an investigation.

For example:

> **KEEP \(h\) CONSTANT → CHANGE \(r\) → PREDICT → TEST**

The teacher then executes the experiment in GeoGebra.

This gives students a **tangible interface for controlling a digital representation**, which is particularly useful for learners who benefit from physical structure and explicit sequencing.

---

# 14. The hybrid approach actually gives you three complementary representations

I think this is the strongest reason not to abandon the digital component.

| Representation                      | What it does particularly well                         |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Physical model/net**              | Establishes what the surfaces actually are             |
| **Dynamic GeoGebra model**          | Shows how surfaces and area change as variables change |
| **Formula/symbolic representation** | Generalises the observed relationship                  |

Rather than choosing one representation, the lesson deliberately asks students to **coordinate all three**.

That is a more sophisticated pedagogical use of technology than simply replacing a cardboard model with a digital one.

---

## 15. And it changes the role of GeoGebra in the lesson

I would therefore revise the original technology decision from:

> **"GeoGebra is optional because students don't have individual devices."**

to:

> **"GeoGebra is a shared classroom investigation instrument, used at moments where dynamic manipulation provides an affordance that static physical models cannot."**

That means I would **not** use GeoGebra throughout the lesson.

I'd use the physical model first because students need to establish *what the surfaces mean*. Then I'd introduce GeoGebra precisely when the mathematical question changes from:

> **"What is the surface of this cylinder?"**

to:

> **"What happens to the surface area when its dimensions change?"**

That division of labour is pedagogically purposeful.

**Physical models answer:** *What is it?*
**Dynamic GeoGebra answers:** *What happens when it changes?*
**Algebra answers:** *Can we generalise it?*

And because students—not the teacher—generate the predictions and decide which parameter to manipulate, the single classroom device can support genuine student agency and analysis without requiring 1:1 technology.

## 4. Reflection

Teachers involved in the work of [El Sayary et al. (2025)](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#elsayary-2025) recommend that the workflow of GenAI-assisted lesson planning be viewed as a continuous cycle of critical evaluations and iterative prompting. My own experience with using GenAI is this task to redesign and improve the Year 9 mathematics lesson is a preparation for actual usage in my future teaching practices. The most significant lesson from this prompt engineering experience is that in order to get useful response, the prompt must contain description of context and intention in a detailed and targeted manner. As commented in [Rappa et al. (2026)](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#rappa-et-al-2026), general-purpose GenAI responses are perhaps too verbose for students’ classroom use. My comment from engaging with ChatGPT in this task is that it will perhaps also be too time-consuming to read carefully in actual teaching practices (given how busy a teacher’s schedule already is). In the future, I will explore more advanced prompt engineering skills for ChatGPT (or other chatbots) to generate shorter and more condensed output.

With the new context drastically different to the one I have actually taught in my practicum, ChatGPT was able to generate a lesson plan with appropriate sequence of learning activities and adequate support for the identified learner groups (EAL/D, ADHD) as well as addressing the particular low-tech context. This sophisticated response stands in stark contrast with the examples given in [van den Berg and du Plessis (2023)](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#vandenberg-2023) where the GenAI response is short enough to be contained in a half-page box. This is a reflection of the remarkable progress in LLM capabilities in the last 3 years since the work of [van den Berg and du Plessis (2023)](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#vandenberg-2023). However, GenAI’s lesson plan should still not be applied uncritically and prompts 2 and 3 targets specific directions of improvements and refinements addressing the learning needs of EAL/D learners and the undue assumption that tech-free activities is the only option.
