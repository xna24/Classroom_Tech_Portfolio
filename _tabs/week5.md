# Portfolio task: Comic of a Learning Moment in a Classroom

## Part 1: Hand-drawn Comic

![Comic of a learning moment in a classroom with stick figures in the style of Randall Munroe's xkcd webcomic](/assets/img/comic.png){: style="display: block; width: 75%; height: auto; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;" }
<p style="width: 75%; margin: 0.35rem auto 0; text-align: center; font-size: 0.75rem; color: #777;">
This comic showing a learning moment is hand-drawn by me using <a href="https://xournalpp.github.io/">Xournal++</a>, an open source app for note-taking. All comic generation tool I could find takes too long to search for the right figures and objects and still require me to hand-draw part of it to make sure the details are not too far off. The style of stick figures in the comic is borrowed from <a href="https://xkcd.com/">Randall Munroe's xkcd webcomic</a>.
</p>

<div class="genai-portal" markdown="1">
- <u>Text in the comic explaining the associated pedagogical approach</u>: Observation of a Year 12 mathematics lesson taught by my mentor teacher during my practicum. Students work in pairs and are given a particle's velocity graphs. They collaborate to sketch corresponding displacement graphs, then come to the whiteboard to enact the motion, graph the displacement, and explain their reasoning to the class. This learning activity reflects a social constructivist pedagogical approach in which students co-construct their mathematical understanding with the teacher and their peers ([Bower, 2017](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#bower-2017)).

- A  digital graphing tool (e.g., GeoGebra or Desmos) can support the same learning outcomes, for instance, by allowing students to dynamically manipulate velocity function and observe the corresponding change in displacement graph. 

- The immediate visual feedback of the dynamic graphing tools could benefit some students who are keen to experiment by generating a vast amount of examples and strengthen their understanding. On the other hand, it is difficult to fully reproduce the social interaction in the fun enactment element of the original lesson. Some benefits of the social learning opportunity would certainly be lost if I were to teach this lesson and replace the activity with one where students sit down and interact only with the digital graphing tools or within small groups.
</div>

### Reflections on Copilot prompting and image generation

> a. What were the stereotypes of teachers, students, and classrooms that were generated?

Without information from the prompt, Copilot assumes that the teacher must be a woman. In reality, the lesson was taught by a male teacher. In all three versions, Copilot have generated some variety of skin tones and hair styles for students. See response to part (c) for more comments.

> b. How accurate was it in terms of following your instructions? What did you have to "correct"?

For the first version, the image generated follows my description rather closely. There are two noteworthy inaccuracies. First, Copilot added its own assumption of the use of the long axis drawn on the whiteboard. In reality, this axis is drawn to help the class understand the relationship between particle motion enactment and displacement graph. Copilot assumed it must be for graphing the displacement graphs themselves, which makes no sense since each pair should use their separate axes. Second, Copilot added a small whiteboard with a description of steps in the activity. It is interesting to note that if this whiteboard was used in my actual classroom, it should be facing the students and be in much larger fonts.

> c. What was the gender, race, age, body-type, etc of the teachers and students? Did you ask for these specifically? Were there any disabled students? Different body types? Where they all "good looking"?

As commented in part (b), Copilot chose a diverse range of race and gender through skin tones and hair styles. However, the facial features and body types of students in all three images are somewhat biased and they all appear "good looking". This is actually a well-documented phenomenon in AI text-to-image generation ([Alon et al., 2026](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#alon-et-al-2026)).

> d. How much work was involved to get the image to look "right"?

I wrote a long and detailed prompt based on my own drawing and the first version of "learning moment" comic made by Copilot already close enough in my opinion. In the second image, Copilot simply inserted iPad use into the learning activity and explained how this technology use "amplify collaboration, visibility of thinking and feedback" in the accompanying text response. Despite the somewhat shallow substitution-level ([Puentedura, 2013](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#puentedura-2013))  integration of technology in this image, I did not invest extensive work in making the image look "right" because I am not entirely sure if there is a right version. As discussed previously, the use of pedagogically-appropriate technology (e.g., dynamic graphing tool using GeoGebra or Desmos) may require an animation rather than a single image to be adequately represented. 

> e. What did the 'personalised learning' image look like in comparison? What did it change?

The third Copilot-generated image added even more components on the iPads to this activity. In this "personalised" vision of the same learning activity, Copilot added a lot of word bubbles explaining pedagogical elements (e.g., "targeted support", "adaptive next step") of what sounds like an adaptive learning system ([Bulger, 2016](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#bulger-2016)). However, the actual interface looks more like a either a learning management platform or "data-driven learning" system described in [Bulger (2016)](/Classroom_Tech_Portfolio/references/#bulger-2016).
